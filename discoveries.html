If you read nothing else

  1. The plan holds, but each job is thin. A $300 job leaves about $57 after pros, dump fees and the cost of the customer. That falls to about $27 if pros get more than 63% or a customer costs more than $40.
  2. The company cannot sell yet. The LLC was dissolved by the state on June 21, 2026, and two brand names have problems (Everline is taken; Bin Buddy is blocked in Utah as an exact name). Reinstating costs about $82; nothing gets filed before that.

The 10 biggest discoveries

Ranked by how much each one changes what we do next.

1

The company that would own the brands is dissolved

What we found
Red Pine Creative Marketing LLC missed its January 31, 2026 renewal. The state dissolved it on June 21, 2026. The engine also had the wrong legal name in it ("Red Pine Creative LLC" belongs to a stranger's old company).
Why it matters
A brand name (DBA) is filed by a live company. Stripe, receipts, insurance and contracts all need a seller in good standing. Nothing can go live until this is fixed.
What we did
Froze all name filings and domain buys. Built your step-by-step go-live kit. Reinstatement is allowed for two years and costs about $82 (your call 10).
Source: case C10 names, go-live kit
2

Our first junk prices lost money

What we found
The $139 / $199 / $279 tiers fail the margin floor in every scenario once insurance and the cost of getting a customer are counted. Even $169 / $249 / $349 only worked if a customer cost under about $21 to $44.
Why it matters
Cheap owner-operators (one charges $150 for an in-home half load) will always beat us on price. Insured two-person crews cost real money.
What we did
Repriced by crew size: $189 / $269 one person, $329 / $449 two people. Pros get up to $95 / $135 / $170 / $215 plus the dump receipt. Positioned as an insured local brand just under the franchises. Software now blocks any job below the floor.
Source: C4 unit economics, red team C4/C5/C9, C11 margin
3

Profit per job is thin, so volume and repeat business are everything

What we found
On a $300 average job: $174 to the pro, $21 dump and supplies, $9 card fees, $6 refund reserve, $8 insurance and tools. That leaves $82, then about $25 to win the customer, so $57 per job (19%).
Why it matters
Small slips erase it. At $50M a year, paying pros 5 more points costs $2.5M; $10 more per customer costs $1.67M; refunds rising from 2% to 5% burns $1.5M.
What we did
Made the margin floor law in code. Every booking records where the customer came from. The money model gets rebuilt from real numbers at 100 and 500 jobs.
Source: round 2 scale economics, C19 synthesis section 2
4

The app is the small part; the company is 14 departments

What we found
Every big failure in this industry came from a missing rule, not a missing feature. DoorDash nearly died in Sept 2013 from a rush it could not switch off. HomeAdvisor paid up to $7.2M for selling weak leads. Instacart paid $60M for fees the screen hid.
Why it matters
We can copy their prevention rules on day one instead of learning them through lawsuits.
What we did
Wrote the company operating system: 14 departments, each with a daily, weekly and monthly rhythm, plus 28 "build before the first live job" items (booking cap, a defect code on every job, a claims desk, a written pro removal and appeal policy).
Source: C19a big platforms, C19 synthesis
5

An all-in-one app like TaskRabbit or Angi would be a mistake

What we found
Angi's lead network revenue fell 72% in 2025 after it let homeowners pick their own pro. Its managed, pre-priced line fell 23%.
Why it matters
Your instinct was right: being the brand that books, charges and stands behind the work beats running an open app.
What we did
Kept the branded-company model. You then chose many brands over one brand (call 13), so the research risks became engineering requirements (see the Model section).
Source: C19d model check (Angi 10-K for 2025)
6

The closest look-alikes got sued, fined or folded

What we found
Handy paid a $6M California settlement (pros must set their own rates) and $2.95M to the FTC and New York in 2025 (earnings claims, hidden fines). Homejoy died in 2015 after a $19 first clean, a 25% cut, customers going around it, and expanding abroad too early.
Why it matters
The legal line is control. If we set the pro's pay, hours and penalties, they start to look like employees.
What we did
Locked in: pros accept, decline or counter each payout (inside a ceiling the margin allows); zero fines ever; scores only change who sees offers first; no hourly or monthly earnings promises in recruiting copy.
Source: C19b home-service marketplaces
7

Selling extras at the door triggers the FTC 3-day cancel rule

What we found
Our first fix ("the office prices extras by phone") failed the red team: the rule covers sales made at the home even when the customer invited us.
Why it matters
Any sale made on site could be cancelled for 3 days and needs a formal notice. A plan offered by the pro at the door counts too.
What we did
Extra junk is charged only at a per-yard rate the customer approves online when booking, with a cap. Pros never quote, sell or collect on site. Plans are offered by email after the job is charged. Same-day extras stay switched off until the attorney answers.
Source: C12 payments, red team C12, C13, C19 section 7.1
8

Customers come from Google and partners, not from paid ads

What we found
Google Local Services Ads cost about $94 to $134 per booked junk job. Industry data: $117.92 per landscaping lead and $46.99 per cleaning lead (leads, not jobs). Facebook Marketplace bans service listings. Nextdoor junk threads in Draper were nearly empty.
Why it matters
Paid leads break the margin floor at our prices. Google allows one business profile per real location with its own staff, so map pins for their own sake are not allowed.
What we did
No paid ads for 60 days. Google Business Profile is the main engine; first jobs come from your network and $5 Craigslist posts. Planned mix at $1M: 40% property-manager and other business accounts, 25% Google, 25% repeat and cross-brand, 10% paid.
Source: C2 demand rounds 1 and 2, round 2 demand at scale, red team C4/C5/C9
9

Money could leak in 26 places; 8 were wide open

What we found
The engine's job ledger is solid but it is not a set of books. Of 26 leak paths: 7 caught, 11 partly caught, 8 open. Examples: the $15 card dispute fee was recorded nowhere; a won dispute stayed a loss; a failed pro payout still showed as paid; sales tax was collected but never set aside.
Why it matters
This is exactly your fear. Sales tax is the state's money held in trust, and spending it is a personal-level problem.
What we did
Designed the accounting department: 5 AI roles (Recorder, Reconciler, Controller, Analyst, Tax and Compliance), a CPA and you. No AI can move money. No role checks its own work. Two bank accounts (operating and held money). Your time: about 10 minutes a week plus month end. The leak fixes are written but not merged yet.
Source: C18 accounting parts A and B, C18 design
10

A human operations lead is needed from the first live job

What we found
Round 1 said "zero staff at $1M". Round 2 disagreed: every company running AI support at scale still had people for escalations, and physical jobs create damage and safety incidents. $1M needs 2 to 3 people besides you; $10M needs 5.
Why it matters
Your 10 hours a week stays the same at $1M, $10M and $50M only if someone else holds day-to-day authority.
What we did
Until revenue pays for it, the ops lead is you or someone you name. That hire is the first thing revenue funds. Claims over $250 go to a human; AI can never deny a claim.
Source: round 2 AI-native org, round 2 risk and claims, C19 section 0

The margin floor (your call 1)

  • One-time jobs: keep at least $25 or 15% of the price, whichever is more, after the customer cost. Target $40 or 20%.
  • Repeat visits: at least $12.50 per planned route hour or 15%, whichever is more.
  • Checked: a $189 junk job keeps $30.64 against a $28.35 floor. A $25 bin visit keeps $5.45 against $3.75. Both pass.
  • Breaks if: a bin pro is paid more than about $13.70 a stop, or a plan customer costs more than about $45.
  • Enforced: the software refuses any quote, pay bump, extra or refund that breaks the floor unless you log an override.

The business model

What survived the research, what you decided, and the risks we are building around.

What holds

  • We are the brand. The customer books and pays us; an insured local pro business does the work. We stand behind it.
  • One back office, many brands. Grab the Gig (pros, pay, dispatch, claims, books) is shared. Each brand is a thin copy: name, phone, site, Google profile, voice.
  • Same offer everywhere. A one-time job, or a repeat plan every 4 or 12 weeks on trash day, paid per visit, cancel online in one step.
  • Pros are businesses, not workers. Owner-led crews of 1 to 5 with their own liability insurance, commercial auto, and workers' comp or the waiver for everyone on the job.
  • Junk first. In-home carry-out from photos, $99 to $399 range. We do not fight $79 curbside pickups or Sandy's free bulk pickup.

What you decided

  • Many brands for volume (call 13, Oct 27). The research recommended one brand first; you rejected it. Volume wins.
  • Think big. Every structure is sized for $1M, $10M and $50M from day one. Sequence the build, never shrink the vision.
  • Copycat brands. A brand may switch an option off, never add its own.
  • Every job meets the margin floor.
  • English and Spanish everywhere, more languages later.
  • $0 until revenue. This is now in tension with launch costs (see P13).

Attorney questions (top ones)

  • Does the FTC 3-day rule reach extras the customer approves on their own phone at home?
  • Does our flow (we set the customer price, pros accept or counter) keep pros as independent businesses under Utah and IRS tests?
  • Is a card hold a "deposit" under Utah rules? Are the $39 and $75 fees enforceable as written?
  • Can we offset a proven damage claim against a future payout, with notice?
  • Does reinstatement cover the time the LLC was dissolved?
  • Utah's marketplace law only protects platforms that charge pros a fee. We assume it does not cover us and will never add a fee to qualify.

CPA questions (top ones)

  • Is residential junk hauling taxable in Utah? Are cancel and trip fees?
  • Do we report revenue as the full job price (we are the seller) or only our cut?
  • Do dump fee reimbursements and tips go on pros' 1099s?
  • One operating account plus one held-money account: enough for several brands?
  • Reinstate the old LLC ($82) or form a new one ($59): any tax reason to prefer one?

Also unknown until real data: signed pro rate cards, quote-to-paid rate, real dump and claim costs, whether a second brand at the same base can get its own Google profile, and how many customers agree to hear from a second brand. Sources: go-live kit part 5, C19 section 11, case board flip tests.

The playbooks we now own

From cases C14 to C17 plus client-site lessons.

  • 9 house rules, including: never invent proof, no A/B tests without about 400 visitors per version.
